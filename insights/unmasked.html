Unmasked at Midlife
Making sense of a
late autism diagnosis
Silvino Rodrigues  · Author · Neurodiversity advocate

I led teams. Made hiring calls.
Built a 30-year career.

And I never knew my own brain was autistic.

The first myth

Why so many were missed
The lens hadn’t been ground yet
Autism as we understand it today is recent. The framework that could finally name adults like me simply didn’t exist when we were children.
2013

Autism was only consolidated into one diagnosis (ASD) with the DSM-5.

Pre-
2013

A co-occurring condition could disqualify an autism diagnosis outright.

Why so many were missed
The ruler was wrong - not the brain
4:1

boys to girls diagnosed in childhood - a gap that nearly vanishes in adult community samples.

The criteria were built on boys

Early theories - like Baron-Cohen’s “extreme male brain” - baked a male picture of autism into the diagnostic markers themselves.

Clinicians were trained to look for masculinity. Girls, women, and skilled maskers were read as something else - or as nothing at all.

Why so many were missed

02
The turning point
What finally makes
us go looking?
What makes us look
Social media: a double edge

The gift

For the first time, adults see their inner experience described out loud - and feel recognised.

The trap

“Autistic Mum”-style advice with no clinical training behind it. Echo chambers harden guesswork into false certainty.

What makes us look
Mainstream media: the sharper harm
Reality TV - like Love on the Spectrum - gives autism a face. But it also gives it a stereotype.
The most damaging move: collapsing a co-occurring intellectual or mental disability and autism into a single person, under one word - “autism.”
What makes us look

Most adults are found
after their child is.

You sit in the assessment for your kid - and the clinician is describing you.

03
Getting an answer
The assessment, briefly
1

You suspect

The process only starts once an adult already believes it may be true.

2

Months of testing

A formal, criteria-based evaluation - not a single appointment.

3

Childhood evidence

School reports and records: traits must be present from early childhood.

4

Family interviews

Parents and siblings fill the memory gaps you can’t see yourself.

Why the childhood evidence? Because autism is developmental - it has to have been there all along.
Before the result
By the time you’re tested, you already suspect

Self-identified

Recognising yourself, without a formal report. Legitimate - and often the honest starting point.

Formally diagnosed

The clinical confirmation of what you already sensed. Legitimate - and, for many, still necessary.

Nobody walks into this cold. So keep one question in mind for the next clip …

04

Courtney hears her diagnosis

“Courtney, Literally”
@CourtneyLiterally

She already knew. So why the tears?

A crushing sense
of loss

Grief - not relief - is often the first response. A devastating realisation.
You mourn the self you might have become, and the life you might have lived.

05
What happens after
Then the shockwave hits

At work

The compensations you built a career on become visible - and suddenly optional. What do you keep? What do you finally put down?

In relationships

Both partners grieve at once. One mourns a self they never knew they had; the other mourns the person they thought they married.

What happens after

And some friends fall away

 

“They were friends with the mask ...
not with me.”

A visceral need to stop performing - the mask has a cost, and now it has a name.

When the performance stops, some connections have nothing left to stand on.

06
What it really means
A late diagnosis is a new life

The child

Is shaped, from the start, with support built around who they are.

The adult

Must re-program a self that was decades in the making. Far harder - and it starts now.

07
If this is you
Support isn’t optional -- it’s critical
But not every psychologist is equipped for neurodivergence. Ask before you commit.

neurodiversity-affirming practice becomes a registration competency for every psychologist in Australia -- driven by years of advocacy from the neurodivergent community.
